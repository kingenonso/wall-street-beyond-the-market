# The talent and funds stage, Part 4b: foreign markets

The owner said "build" on 31 October 2026 for Part 4b, the second piece of the currency desk, global markets and new industries.
The owner's earlier answers set the shape:
- foreign shares trade on foreign exchanges styled on New York, London and Frankfurt;
- the countries and cities are real, but every company, exchange and central bank name is invented;
- about 30 foreign companies;
- the Nigerian arms of foreign firms are listed in Lagos.

Part 4b covers everything on the list for it:
- three foreign exchanges with their own hours and indexes;
- 30 foreign companies;
- three foreign economies, each with a central bank;
- buying foreign shares with dollars, pounds and euros;
- Nigerian arms of foreign firms, with dividends trapped by a dollar shortage and parents who can leave;
- ETFs of foreign shares on the Broad Street Exchange.

Nothing was dropped or put off. Following the owner's standing rule, nothing new was added to the sidebar:
- **The markets abroad:** a "World markets" tab inside Markets.
- **The economies abroad:** a panel on the Economy page.
- **A Nigerian arm's parent:** shown on the company's own page.
- **Big moves:** these come as pop-ups.

## Things worth knowing, said plainly

1. **Three exchanges abroad, each on its own city's clock.**

   | Exchange | City | Currency | Index |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Hudson Stock Exchange | New York | dollars | Hudson 10 |
   | Thames Exchange | London | pounds | Thames 10 |
   | Rhine-Main Exchange | Frankfurt | euros | Rhine-Main 10 |

   - They open and close at their real local hours, shown in Lagos time. Summer and winter time are followed, so New York opens at 14:30 Lagos time in October and 15:30 in December.
   - Like Broad Street, they trade every day of the week, because the game's calendar is compressed.
   - Nobody can deal while an exchange is closed.
2. **Thirty companies, ten on each exchange.**
   - They cover technology, medicines, cars, oil and gas, banks, food, household goods, mining, shops, drinks, telecoms, insurance, shipping, luxury goods, chemicals and power.
   - Every quarter each company reports its sales, profit and dividend.
3. **How their prices move.**
   - Prices move every ten minutes while the exchange is open: with the market as a whole, with the company's own news, and a little each day towards what the share is worth.
   - What a share is worth comes from its profits over the last year and from its country's long interest rate. When rates rise, a share is worth fewer years of profit, and a fast-growing technology company loses the most.
   - A quarter's results move the price 80% of the way at once.
   - The secret path of prices is never sent to players.
4. **Three economies abroad.**
   - **The economies.** The United States, the United Kingdom and the euro area each have growth and inflation every quarter.
   - **Their central banks.** The Union Reserve, the Sterling Monetary Board and the Continental Central Bank each set their rate by a published rule: up when inflation runs above 2% or growth runs hot, part of the way each quarter, in quarter-point steps. Their next move is never sent.
   - **What they now do to Nigeria:**
     - Growth abroad above its usual pace lifts the oil price.
     - America's rate is now what "lending abroad" pays, so foreign investors in Nigeria want more when it is low and less when it is high. Eurobond yields and the naira future follow it too.
     - The pound and the euro rise against the dollar when their rates rise against America's.
     - Dearer oil raises inflation abroad.
5. **Buying foreign shares.**
   - **How to pay.** Players buy with their dollars, pounds or euros from the Currencies tab. The brokers abroad sell 0.15% above the price and buy 0.15% below it, and a big deal moves the price.
   - **Limits.** At most $2 million's worth in one deal, and at most 0.1% of a company's shares a day for each account.
   - **For an empire.** Its head, Investment Banker or Asset Manager deals for it, within their trading limit and never while the Risk Manager has frozen them.
   - **Gains, two ways.** A holding shows what it made on the share and what it made on the currency, separately: when the naira falls, foreign money is worth more naira.
   - **Dividends.** They are paid in the currency, after 15% tax kept abroad, and the price falls by the dividend.
   - **Net worth.** Foreign shares count in net worth at what the brokers and the street would pay.
6. **Nigerian arms of foreign companies.**

   | Nigerian company | Parent abroad |
   |---|---|
   | Amaka Household Products | Hollis & Grant |
   | Tamuno Breweries | Harrowgate Brewers |
   | Sango Networks | Meridian Telecom Africa |
   | Delta Creek Gas | Seine Energies |
   | Ayo Dairy | Polder Dairy Group |

   - **The parent's stake.** The founders' stake in each is recorded as the parent's. Nothing else about the company changed, and its free float in the indexes is the same.
   - **Votes and rights.** The parent sits with the board in votes, and takes up rights issues with its naira.
   - **Dividends waiting for dollars.** The parent's dividends wait in naira until there are dollars to send them home. Between them, the parents may send at most 10% of the Central Bank's reserves home a quarter. That falls to nothing as reserves drop from two and a half quarters of imports to one. A floating naira lets them leave at the street rate.
   - **When a parent leaves.** It may decide to leave when two years of dividends are stuck here, or when the naira has lost 40% in two years. Each quarter with such a reason there is a 15% chance. Then:
     - for two quarters, empires may buy pieces of its stake at the market price, staying under 30%;
     - any takeover offer gets its shares;
     - what is left goes to the pension funds at 20% below the market.
   - **The asking price.** The "market price" here is the highest of the last trade, the last session's settled price and the five-session average, so one small trade cannot pull it down.
7. **ETFs of foreign shares in naira.**
   - The computer's Broad Street Asset Management runs three: the Hudson 10, Thames 10 and Rhine-Main 10 ETFs. Each copies its exchange's index counted in naira, which appears among the other indexes.
   - Their naira go abroad through the street to buy the shares, so money pouring into them weakens the naira a little.
   - Units are made and unmade against cash only, never a basket, and whoever makes or unmakes them pays what dealing abroad costs.
   - Households keep 3% of their index money in them, more when the street's premium is high.
   - An Asset Manager can launch their own ETF of a foreign index.
8. **Where it shows.**
   - The World markets tab.
   - The Economy page.
   - A Nigerian arm's company page.
   - Seven new kinds of news: central bank moves, growth abroad, a big day's fall or rise, a parent leaving, a parent gone, dividends stuck.
   - Pop-ups for the same, and for your foreign dividends.

## Found and fixed

**By the loophole tests:**
1. A leaving parent's stake at 10% below the market could be bought and sold straight back on the exchange. It now sells at the market price.
2. With only half of a quarter's news in the price at once, the rest came in day after day for anyone to collect. Now 80% is priced at once, and the daily pull is gentler.

**By the independent review:**
1. One small trade between two players could set the price a leaving parent sold its stake at. The price is now the highest of three measures.
2. Dealing abroad for an empire skipped the trading limit and the Risk Manager's freeze. The currency desk's empire deals did too, a gap from Part 4a. Both are now guarded, and nobody can deal in a company abroad the other way to their own empire on the same day.
3. The empires that bought a parent's stake were named to everybody. Now only "empires", "the pension funds" or "a takeover offer" are named.
4. A dealer unmaking foreign-ETF units for cash was paid slightly short.
5. Buy prices were rounded down, not up.
6. Households making foreign-ETF units took a little value from the holders already in.
7. Interest rates abroad moved the pound and euro a hundred times too little.
8. Oil moved inflation abroad ten times too little.
9. A pop-up about "₦0.00 waiting for dollars" appeared when nothing was waiting.

## Honest limits

- Foreign companies are simpler than Nigerian ones: sales, profit and dividends, with no wages, loans or balance sheets.
- Their prices are not set by an order book. Players deal with the brokers abroad at the price.
- Prices abroad in the import bill still rise a steady 2.5% a year. America's inflation now drives foreign investors and the naira's fair value, but not import costs.
- Nigerian companies listing abroad, and cross-border takeovers, come later as planned (with the IPO roadshow and company money moves).
- Investment funds that players run (Part 2) cannot buy foreign shares directly.
