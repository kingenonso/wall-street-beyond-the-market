## Things worth knowing, said plainly

1. **Two prices for one dollar.**
   - The **official rate** is set by the Central Bank at each quarter's close.
   - The **street rate** is where anybody can buy or sell at once.
   - The gap between them is public, and it is the clearest sign of how short of dollars the country is.
2. **How the Central Bank runs the naira.** These rules are published on the page.
   - While it **manages** the naira, it moves the official rate a quarter of the way towards the market each quarter.
   - When the street runs 5% or more above the official rate, and it has at least two quarters of imports in reserve, it **defends** the naira: it sells dollars on the street through the coming quarter. The naira paid for them leave circulation, and that strengthens the naira.
   - When the gap reaches 15% and its reserves are under two and a half quarters of imports, it **devalues**: the official rate jumps to the market's.
   - Under one quarter of imports, it lets the naira **float**: the official rate follows the street. Once reserves are back over three quarters of imports, it manages the naira again.
   - When reserves run low, the Government borrows dollars abroad with a **Eurobond**, at most once a year.
3. **The reserves.**
   - The Central Bank's dollars are real money in the ledger.
   - Oil sold abroad brings most of them in. They pay for part of what companies buy abroad, and foreign investors' money passes through them in both directions.
   - Dollars, pounds and euros come into existence only from the rest of the world. The Central Bank makes naira only when it buys dollars, and takes them out of existence when it sells. The ledger audit checks both.
4. **Your own dollars, pounds and euros.**
   - Players and empires hold them like naira. The empire's head or its Investment Banker deals for the empire.
   - **The street** deals at once, 1.5% either side of its middle rate. Big deals push it up or down for the day.
   - **The official window** gives dollars at the official rate, in turn, from a daily allocation of the reserves. The limit is $50,000 a quarter for a player and $500,000 for an empire. The naira (and a tenth more) are held until the request is filled. Requests still waiting at the quarter's close are handed back.
   - **No round-tripping.** Dollars from the window cannot be sold on the street or to a bureau for two quarters. The window buys them back no dearer than it sold them. Buying cheap at the official rate to sell dear on the street is a crime against the game, so the game blocks it.
5. **Currency forwards.**
   - A forward fixes today the naira you will pay for a dollar in a coming quarter. At each close only the difference against the official rate changes hands.
   - A bank prices it from where the official rate is heading under the published rules, so a devaluation everybody can see coming is already in the price.
   - Companies with dollar debts hedge 30% of their interest this way, automatically.
6. **Bureaux de change.**
   - An empire can open one for a ₦25 million licence. It needs ₦200 million behind it.
   - It sets its own buying and selling prices around the street's middle rate.
   - Households, and players, deal with the cheapest. Every deal of $10,000 or more is reported to the regulator automatically.
   - A bureau buys its dollars on the street. It can never use the official window, and it cannot open while its empire holds dollars from the window.
7. **Money sent home.**
   - Nigerians abroad send about 40% as much as the country imports.
   - They send more at Easter and at Christmas, more when the street pays more naira, and 5% more each year.
   - Money-transfer companies carry it. The computer runs three, and an empire can run one for a ₦50 million licence.
   - Families choose by fee and speed. Faster costs more to run. The money reaches households in naira and eases the street.
8. **Companies and the dollar.**
   - Importers already paid more, and exporters earned more, when the naira fell.
   - Now the largest oil and gas companies, and the largest telecoms company, also have dollar debts (Eurobonds).
   - Each quarter those debts are revalued at the official rate. The change is a new "Currency loss" line in their accounts (a gain when the naira rises), and their books still balance to the kobo.
9. **Eurobonds.**
   - These are dollar bonds of the Government and of those companies, bought and sold with dollars at a fair price, ±0.5%.
   - The price falls when lenders see more risk in Nigeria (lower reserves), and it includes the interest built up during the quarter.
   - Interest is paid in dollars every quarter. If a payment falls short, the bond is in default and is priced as one.
10. **The dollar backlog.**
    - While the naira is managed, foreign investors who want to leave get at most 5% of the reserves a quarter. The rest waits.
    - The backlog is public. It makes the remaining investors keener to sell, and it widens the street's premium.
11. **Where it shows.**
    - **Net worth:** foreign money and Eurobonds count at what the street would pay.
    - **News:** eight new kinds of news.
    - **Pop-ups:** a devaluation, a float, the Central Bank stepping in, reserves running low, a wide gap, the backlog, a new Eurobond or a default, and your window request being filled.

## Honest limits

- The economy's own figures still use one exchange rate: the market's. The official rate matters for the window, the reserves, dollar debts, Eurobonds and forwards, but not for what companies pay for imports.
- The pound and the euro move against the dollar by chance each quarter. Their own economies arrive with Part 4b.
- A bureau trades from its empire's own money (the same account), as a small business would.
