The owner approved Part 2 with "build" on 17 October 2026 (Part 1, the newsroom, was approved on 16 October with "1. lets

## Things worth knowing, said plainly

1. **Information is a thing in the world now.** Every piece (a tip, a source's hint, a rumour, a leak) has a record:
   what it is about, whether it is a rumour, a lead or verified, how reliable its source is, when it stops being useful,
   and who held it and since when. Whether it is true, and what it came from, only the server knows.
2. **The Sealed Tip.** Every player gets one each real day, matched to their build (a journalist's may be about a hidden
   pact or money taken inside an empire; a lawyer's about pacts; the others' about companies). About a third are about
   something real and still to come, a third are false, a third are true but already public. It never says a day or a size.
   Once opened it is useful for four hours. Extra tips cannot be bought. About one in ten comes from someone inside a
   company: trading on that while it is useful is dealing on confidential information, and it is kept for the courts
   (Stage 11), laid only at the door of whoever placed the order.
3. **Checking a piece** costs ₦50,000 and is right 70% of the time, plus half a percent a level (at most 95%). A forecast
   (where the Central Bank will go, where the Government will build) cannot be known until it is settled: checking one is a
   coin toss. One player's check never changes what others see.
4. **The Intelligence Market.** Holders sell copies of a piece at a price they set (₦10,000 to ₦50 million). A buyer sees
   what it is about, its status and its source's reliability before buying, and reads it only after. For a hidden pact or
   theft, even the names stay hidden until bought (the names are the claim). A piece can also be given, or leaked, to a
   Financial Journalist.
5. **Sources.** A journalist builds a source inside a company, or in the ministries, over two quarters; two sources, one more
   for each ten levels, at most five; one new one a quarter. A source is right about 80% of the time and can be asked once a
   real day. Printing its hint before it is borne out burns it three times in ten; selling its hint, twice in ten.
6. **Rumours move prices.** A journalist may put one piece they hold into a story as an unconfirmed report. It is printed
   as unconfirmed, read by seven in ten of those who would read a confirmed story, and moves investors half as far, and
   only as far as they trust the author. When it is settled, the author's credibility rises if it was borne out; if it was
   wrong, the story must be corrected within a day (as in Part 1) and investors take back what they marked. A piece that is
   already public moves nothing: it is old news. The "Rumours on the record" page lists every unconfirmed report, what
   investors made of it, and how it turned out.
7. **Investigations.** A journalist opens a file on an empire or a charity and searches a day at a time (one search a day
   on each target, whatever file it is in). Documents turn up only if something is really hidden: money taken inside an
   empire and dressed up in its books, an unlawful pact, or a charity paying its own. With three documents and a second
   source, the exposé runs: the theft is proved (a case opens, as after an audit), the pact comes to light, or the charity is
   shown up. A ₦2 million prize from the paper, five points of reputation and five of credibility. An exposé cannot be
   made up: nothing can be printed without the documents and the second source.
8. **The empire's press desk** (its head, or its Financial Journalist):
   - the **press office** answers other journalists' questions for the empire;
   - **crisis statements**: when something comes out, the empire has 24 hours to admit it, say it regrets it, or deny it.
     Admitting what is true costs one point; denying it, six; saying nothing, four; denying something false gains one.
     For an unconfirmed report, what the answer does to the empire's name waits until the report is borne out or proved
     wrong, so answering can never tell anyone whether a hidden thing is so. A computer-run empire says only that it does not
     comment, whatever is so;
   - **who has been looking at us**, from public traces only (questions asked, stories printed);
   - the **fact-checking desk**: the journalist checks a tip a member holds before the Asset Manager trades on it;
   - **leaks**: a member can pass something true about their own empire to a journalist (a holding, a plan, a hidden pact).
     When it is printed, the empire suffers a leak and must answer for it; a printed pact comes to light. The head or the
     journalist can hunt each leak once; the hunter alone learns who it was (a leaker hunting their own leak learns nothing
     and leaves the hunt for the head). A hidden pact may also leak to the press on its own, one chance in twenty a quarter;
   - **spin, or refuse**: the head may ask the journalist to answer a story with a reply that twists it. Refusing keeps a
     clean name (a point of reputation). Going along helps the empire by two points for now, but one in four such replies
     comes out each quarter (or when a player raises it), and then it costs the empire four points and the journalist five
     of reputation and ten of credibility. An honest reply is always open;
   - **planting a rumour**: a desk can start one rumour a day about a company or a rival empire. Nothing hidden is read to
     start it, its starter cannot check it, and whether it was true is settled against what really happens afterwards.
9. **A widely read story moves the reputation of the empire it names** by a point its way (within the six-points-a-day
   press cap from Part 1). With the crisis statements above, the press now moves an empire's name, which Part 1 could not.
10. **Intelligence work.** The Financial Journalist is also the Economic Intelligence Officer: six new pieces of work, from
    Level 33 to 47 (how far to trust a source and a check; when an exposé may run; reading the Central Bank; foreign money;
    oil, the naira and energy at home; which company is closest to trouble). Each build also sees its part of the public
    record laid out on the Intelligence page (lawyers: fines and pacts that came to light; accountants: restated accounts
    and profits whose cash fell; bankers: disclosed stakes and takeovers; risk and asset managers: unconfirmed reports and
    how they turned out, and the day's severe events).
11. **Charities.** A player, or an empire through its head, founds one with at least ₦1 million, for a cause in a city;
    anyone can give; its trustee grants to the cause (the money reaches the city's households) or to a person. Reputation
    comes only from what reaches the cause (an empire, a point for each ₦25 million, at most three a quarter; a player, a
    point for each ₦5 million, at most two). An empire's giving stays within what its head may sign for in a quarter, and an
    empire's charity is always run by its current head. Grants to people connected to the founder or the donors are kept on
    the record; the Money Watch privately flags a charity when they pass a quarter of its grants, or when most of its money
    goes to named people. A journalist can investigate a charity. Prosecution waits for Stages 11 and 12.
12. **Media titles at the Auction House.** Five small titles, one in each city, began with the Government, which sells one
    each quarter: The Garden City Courier (Port Harcourt), The Harmattan Post (Kano), Capital Bulletin (Abuja), The Evening
    Drum (Lagos) and Aba Market Voice (a radio station in Aba). Each brings in ₦40 a quarter for each regular reader and ₦2
    for each reader of the stories printed in it, paid by its city's households; three-fifths go on staff, paper and
    print, and the rest is paid to the owner. A journalist who owns a title, or sits in the empire that owns it, can print in
    it; its audience grows with what is printed. A title is worth about five years' profit; the big media houses bid up to
    95% of that. It is bought outright (no partner, no mortgage). An owner's lowest price, and an empire's bid, are at most
    twice what it is worth.
13. **Honest limits.**
    - A claim about a hidden theft or pact is settled only when the thing comes out. One that never comes out stays "not
      yet settled" for good: settling it any other way would give the secret away. So an empire that rightly denies a
      false report of a theft does not get its point back unless something settles it.
    - In the ten-year run, the player empire neglected its filings and duties, so the existing regulator fines (a Stage 7
      rule) took most of its reputation; a theft exposed and its charity shown up took the rest. Its name hit zero by the
      end of the sixth year. That is the rules working, but it shows how hard a neglected empire falls.
    - Planted rumours and dealing on confidential information are recorded but not yet prosecuted: the courts come with
      Stage 11.

## The ten loophole questions (`tests/test_stage10b_loopholes.py`)

1. Can any page send a piece's truth, what it came from, or who planted it? No.
2. Can a player get a second Sealed Tip in a day, or buy one? No.
3. Can a piece be used by someone who does not hold it, or once it has run out or been proved wrong? No.
4. Can the market be gamed (buy twice, buy one's own, buy what has expired, list without limit)? No.
5. Can an exposé be made up, run twice, or run after someone else got there first? No.
6. Can someone answer an empire's crisis who does not speak for it, late, or twice? No.
7. Can anyone but the hunter learn who leaked; can a hunt run twice; can a member leak what is not so? No.
8. Can a charity pay its trustee, or hide a grant to insiders from the record? No.
9. Can a title be bought on borrowed or shared money, by its owner, or printed in by someone who does not own it? No.
10. Can trading on confidential information escape the record? No.

## The review's findings, all fixed (`tests/test_stage10b_review.py`)

1. Crisis answers could give away whether a hidden thing was so: a computer-run empire now says nothing, whatever is so,
   and an answer about an unconfirmed report counts only once the report is settled.
2. A planted rumour read the hidden record, so it could be used to test any target: it now reads nothing, its starter
   cannot check it, and it does not help an investigation.
3. Dropping and reopening a file gave extra searches: one search a day on each target now.
4. An empire's head could move its money out through a charity or an overpriced title: both now stay within the head's
   signing limit, a title's price is held to twice its worth, and an empire's charity is run by its current head.
5. A leaker could use up the hunt for their own leak: their hunt now finds nothing and leaves the hunt for the head.
6. Old news printed as a "confirmed" report moved prices fully: it is old news now.
7. A wrong report could escape correction if the story had been corrected before: it now needs its own.
8. A forecast's stored truth could disagree with how it was settled: forecasts are unknown until the close.
9. Dealing on confidential information was laid at every member's door: only at whoever placed the order.
10. Reports already borne out stayed "not yet settled": they are settled at once.
11. A charity's name could carry figures into print: names are in words.
12. Two small races (leaks, replies): locked.
13. The website showed "for my empire" to members who are not the head, and a charity search allowed too short a name.
Minor: a pact or theft piece showed its names on the market before it was bought: only its topic now.
Found by the ten-year run: checking or buying without the cash crashed instead of refusing plainly.
